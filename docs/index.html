Abstract

Plumbing insecurity—a critical dimension of water insecurity—refers to households lacking complete indoor plumbing, with significant implications for public health, hygiene, and infrastructure equity. While often taken for granted in developed countries, inadequate access to safe indoor plumbing elevates the risk of waterborne diseases, impedes hygiene, and disproportionately affects vulnerable populations across income levels. Tracking plumbing insecurity helps identify emerging public health risks, guide resource allocation, and inform equitable infrastructure development. This study analyzes U.S. county-level plumbing insecurity using 2022–2023 ACS data, employing bar plots, choropleth maps, and spatial clustering (Getis-Ord G) to detect geographic hotspots and coldspots. Using a Queen contiguity method with a 20 km snap distance and a Z-score threshold of 1.96, we analyzed 673 counties for spatial clustering. Results identified hotspot clusters of worsening conditions primarily in New Jersey, Louisiana, and Michigan, while coldspot clusters showing improvement were most concentrated in Virginia and Ohio. Persistent regional disparities remain, with plumbing insecurity concentrated in Arizona and New Mexico—especially Apache, McKinley, and Navajo counties—as well as notable insecurity in Alaska’s Fairbanks North Star and Matanuska-Susitna counties, highlighting ongoing infrastructural challenges and the need for targeted policy interventions.

Question 1:

Top counties had the highest plumbing insecurity in 2022 and 2023?

Introduction


These two datasets provide information on the lack of complete indoor plumbing across U.S. counties in 2022 and 2023. The 2022 dataset includes data from 848 counties, while the 2023 dataset covers 854 counties, reflecting increased reporting in 2023. Each dataset contains seven variables: county ID (geoid), county name, geographic boundaries (geometry), year, total population, number of households lacking plumbing, and the percent lacking plumbing. This analysis focuses on identifying the top counties with the highest plumbing insecurity in each year and examining the geographic distribution of these counties to understand regional patterns of indoor plumbing leakage in the United States.

Approach


We will identify and visualize the U.S. counties with the highest levels of plumbing insecurity in 2022 and 2023. We first cleaned the data using the dplyr package by sorting counties in descending order of percent lacking plumbing and selecting the top 10 counties. We then created a new variable to classify counties as having greater than 1% or less than 1% plumbing insecurity for color coding in plots. For visualization, we used ggplot2 to create bar graphs displaying the top 10 counties with the highest plumbing insecurity percentages in 2022 and 2023, colored by insecurity level. To visualize the geographic distribution, we loaded a U.S. county shapefile using the tigris package (the geometry information in the datasets were stored as character class strings rather than usable spatial geometry objects), merged it with our top 10 data by county geoid, and created a choropleth map.

Discussion


Both the 2022 and 2023 maps show that Arizona and New Mexico counties dominate the highest plumbing insecurity group (>1%), suggesting persistent disparities in plumbing access across the Southwest. Among these two states, Apache County (Arizona), McKinley County (New Mexico), and Navajo County (Arizona) consistently ranked among the top four counties with the highest plumbing insecurity in both years, indicating enduring infrastructure and access challenges in these regions. Additionally, Alaska counties, including Fairbanks North Star Borough and Matanuska-Susitna Borough, consistently appeared with notable insecurity levels, highlighting infrastructure limitations in remote areas. Overall, plumbing insecurity in the U.S. remains concentrated in specific Southwestern and Alaskan counties, reflecting long-standing infrastructural inequities in these regions.

Question 2:

How did the plumbing insecurity change from 2022 to 2023 across U.S. counties, and where do significant spatial clusters (hotspots and coldspots) of worsening or improving plumbing access exist?

Introduction

This analysis examines how plumbing insecurity has changed across U.S. counties between 2022 and 2023. Plumbing insecurity is defined as the percentage of households lacking complete indoor plumbing, a key indicator of basic water access. Using data from the American Community Survey (ACS), we aim to measure the year-over-year change in plumbing access and determine whether there are spatial clusters—groups of neighboring counties—that have significantly improved (coldspots) or worsened (hotspots). The 2022 dataset includes 848 counties, while the 2023 dataset includes 854 counties. For this analysis, we restrict the data to counties that are present in both years and have no missing values for the percent_lacking_plumbing variable, allowing for a valid and consistent change calculation.

Approach

After cleaning and filtering the data to retain only counties with complete plumbing insecurity data for both 2022 and 2023, we calculated the year-over-year change in plumbing insecurity as the difference in percentages between the two years. To explore spatial patterns in these changes, we performed Local Getis-Ord G* analysis using the spdep package, which identifies statistically significant local clusters of high or low values within geographic neighborhoods.

To visualize these spatial clusters, we created a choropleth map using the tmap package. This map colors counties based on their hotspot, coldspot, or non-significant status, providing an intuitive spatial overview of where plumbing insecurity is worsening or improving. Choropleth maps are ideal for this purpose because they convey geographic patterns clearly and facilitate regional comparisons.

In addition to the spatial visualization, we conducted a hyperparameter sweep across key model parameters—specifically snap distance and contiguity type—to understand how these choices affect the detection of spatial clusters. This analysis was visualized using a line plot showing the number and significance of identified clusters as a function of snap distance. The line plot helps illustrate how parameter tuning impacts model sensitivity and potential overfitting, making it easier to select optimal settings for reliable spatial inference.

Together, these two complementary plots — a choropleth map and a hyperparameter sweep line plot — allow us to both identify meaningful spatial patterns in plumbing insecurity change and validate the robustness of our spatial clustering methodology.

Spatial Cluster Classification

Each county was labeled based on the Getis-Ord G* z-score as follows:

  • Hotspot (significantly worsening): z ≥ 1.96
  • Coldspot (significantly improving): z ≤ -1.96
  • Not significant (no spatial clustering): -1.96 < z < 1.96

These classifications were used to identify clusters of counties with statistically meaningful changes in plumbing insecurity between 2022 and 2023.

# Hyperparameters
queen_setting <- TRUE
snap_distance <- 20000
z_threshold <- 1.96

# ────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Load and clean data
water_2022_clean <- read.csv(here("Water_insecurity", "water_insecurity_2022.csv")) %>%
  filter(!is.na(percent_lacking_plumbing)) %>%
  mutate(geoid = as.character(geoid))

water_2023_clean <- read.csv(here("Water_insecurity", "water_insecurity_2023.csv")) %>%
  filter(!is.na(percent_lacking_plumbing)) %>%
  mutate(geoid = as.character(geoid))

# Filter to common GEOIDs
common_geoids <- intersect(water_2022_clean$geoid, water_2023_clean$geoid)
water_2022_filtered <- water_2022_clean %>% filter(geoid %in% common_geoids)
water_2023_filtered <- water_2023_clean %>% filter(geoid %in% common_geoids)

# ────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Join to shapefile
counties_sf <- counties(cb = TRUE, year = 2022, class = "sf")

# Filter to continental US counties (exclude AK '02', HI '15', PR '72', etc.)
continental_states <- setdiff(unique(counties_sf$STATEFP), c("02", "15", "72"))
counties_sf_continental <- counties_sf %>%
  filter(STATEFP %in% continental_states)

total_counties_continental <- nrow(counties_sf_continental)

counties_2022 <- counties_sf_continental %>%
  left_join(water_2022_filtered %>%
              select(geoid, percent_lacking_plumbing_2022 = percent_lacking_plumbing),
            by = c("GEOID" = "geoid"))

counties_2023 <- counties_sf_continental %>%
  left_join(water_2023_filtered %>%
              select(geoid, percent_lacking_plumbing),
            by = c("GEOID" = "geoid"))

# ────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Calculate change and project
spatial_data <- counties_2023 %>%
  left_join(counties_2022 %>% st_drop_geometry() %>%
              select(GEOID, percent_lacking_plumbing_2022),
            by = "GEOID") %>%
  mutate(change = percent_lacking_plumbing - percent_lacking_plumbing_2022) %>%
  filter(!is.na(change))

# Project to Albers Equal Area
spatial_data <- st_transform(spatial_data, crs = 5070)

# ────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Create neighbors
nb <- poly2nb(spatial_data, queen = queen_setting, snap = snap_distance)

# Identify and exclude counties with no neighbors
no_neighbors <- which(card(nb) == 0)
num_no_neighbors <- length(no_neighbors)

if (num_no_neighbors > 0) {
  spatial_data <- spatial_data[-no_neighbors, ]
  nb <- poly2nb(spatial_data, queen = queen_setting, snap = snap_distance)
}

# Total counties analyzed after exclusions
total_analyzed <- nrow(spatial_data)

# ────────────────────────────────────────────────

Discussion

The choropleth map of Getis-Ord G* clusters reveals that year-over-year changes in plumbing insecurity are not randomly distributed across U.S. counties but are instead spatially patterned. Statistically significant hotspots—areas where plumbing access worsened—were most concentrated in New Jersey, Louisiana, and Michigan, while coldspots—areas of improvement—were primarily found in Virginia and Ohio. These patterns likely reflect regional differences in infrastructure funding, policy implementation, or socioeconomic vulnerability.

To evaluate how spatial model parameters interacted with the data’s topology, a hyperparameter sweep was performed across three variables: contiguity type (Queen vs. Rook), snap distance, and z-score threshold. Among these, snap distance had the most substantial impact on the clustering results. A 20,000-meter threshold produced coherent clusters without overfitting, while higher distances led to overly broad neighborhood definitions and reduced spatial precision. The Queen contiguity setting—capturing both shared edges and corners—was used, but contiguity type had minimal influence on output quality. The z-score threshold of ±1.96 (p ≤ 0.05) effectively distinguished statistically significant clusters.

These findings demonstrate that significant clusters of change in plumbing insecurity do exist and are geographically meaningful—but only when spatial model parameters are carefully tuned. The hyperparameter sweep, akin to methods used in machine learning, revealed that snap distance was the most influential factor in detecting these patterns. When set too high, the model effectively overfit, diluting meaningful local trends by merging unrelated counties into large, unrealistic neighborhoods. By selecting an optimal 20 km threshold, we improved model interpretability and spatial resolution without compromising statistical validity. This outcome highlights the value of treating spatial analysis with the same rigor applied in machine learning workflows—where thoughtful parameter tuning is essential to uncovering accurate and actionable insights.
